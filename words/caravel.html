Read the full story

Caravel’s story begins in Greek: kárabos meant “stag beetle, crayfish.” Named horned beetles and crayfish; Aristotle's History of Animals uses it for the spiny lobster. By c. 630, the word had taken hold in Late Latin as carabus. Isidore of Seville's Etymologiae defines carabus as a small boat of wicker covered in raw hide. From there it passed into Portuguese as caravela by 1400s. Portugal's light, lateen-rigged ship of discovery; Bartolomeu Dias rounded the Cape of Good Hope in 1488 with two caravels. It reached English by 1520s as caravel. Came via French caravelle; two of Columbus's three ships in 1492, the Niña and the Pinta, were caravels. Today, caravel means a small, fast Spanish or Portuguese sailing ship of the 1400s-1600s.
