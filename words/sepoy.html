Read the full story

Sepoy’s story begins in Persian: sipah meant “army, soldier.” Meant "army"; sipahi meant "soldier, horseman". By 1500s, the word had taken hold in Urdu as sipahi. Kept the military sense in Mughal India. From there it passed into Portuguese as sipae by 1600s. Colonial traders used it for locally recruited soldiers. It reached English by 1717 as sepoy. Adopted by the British to describe Indian soldiers serving under their command. Today, sepoy means historically, a native Indian soldier serving under British or other European military command.
