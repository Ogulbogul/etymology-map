Knout’s story begins in Old Norse: knútr meant “knot.” 'knot'; usually traced to the Varangian Norsemen who helped found the Rus' state from the 800s. By 1600s, the word had taken hold in Russian as knut. A knotted leather whip; Peter the Great's son Alexei died in 1718 after being beaten with it on his father's orders. It reached English by 1670s as knout. English kept the French spelling; the knout stayed a byword for tsarist cruelty even after Nicholas I abolished it as a judicial punishment in 1845. Today, knout means a whip formerly used to flog criminals in Russia.
